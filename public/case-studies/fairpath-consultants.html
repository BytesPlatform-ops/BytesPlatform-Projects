The business

The equipment is specialised. The site is where it becomes something a person can understand

FairPath Consultants LLC is a Gardena, California supplier of adaptive and assistive devices. Its About page states the business in one line, and it sets the frame: “This is not about the founder, it is about enabling communities in need of assistive and adaptive devices.” Behind that sit a shop, two product categories, a fourteen-entry FAQ, a capability statement, a blog and a contact page.

Reduce that to an online store and most of the offer disappears. The published offer is two specific products, a standing bed and a portable bedside shower system, sold with white-glove delivery, a stated warranty, a buy-back programme and a membership, to individuals, caregivers and buyers in the “government, healthcare, and education sectors.”

Two readers, one cart

The challenge

Unfamiliar equipment, two kinds of buyer

A standing bed and a bedside shower are function-specific devices with no shared vocabulary between the person who needs them and the office that buys them. The purchase may be made for someone in the middle of care. Five pressures shaped the build.

The need is specific and the product is unfamiliar

Someone needs a way to stand safely from bed or bathe without being moved. They do not know the product is called a standing bed. The site has to be found by the need and name the device plainly.

The vocabulary is contested

Adaptive, assistive, mobility aid. The site uses two of these as its categories and has to explain the difference before a visitor can choose one.

Two buyers, two sets of questions

A caregiver asks what it does and whether it can be returned. A procurement officer asks who manufactures it and how fast it ships. One page has to answer both.

Standards are in the room

International standards and safety tests come up in every institutional purchase. The site has to cite what the manufacturer documents, in the manufacturer’s words, and nothing more.

The site’s own accessibility is the credential

A supplier of assistive devices whose site is hard to use has lost the argument before it starts. Headings, alt text and link names are trust signals here, not housekeeping.

The organising principle: name each product in plain words, define adaptive and assistive in the blog before selling into them, answer the procurement questionnaire in public so the institutional buyer’s file is already written, cite every standard in the manufacturer’s words, publish the warranty, delivery, return and buy-back terms where the price is, and make the site itself operable by the people it serves.

What we built

01 / 07

Plain names, defined words, published terms

Two function-named products with a price, a sale and a SKU on the shop card, a blog that defines the category, a fourteen-entry FAQ shaped as a procurement questionnaire, a capability statement page and a cart. By the time someone adds a device to the cart or asks for a consultation, they have searched a need they may not have a product word for, learned the category’s terms, found a product named for what it does, read what happens after delivery, checked the manufacturer and standards documentation, and decided the company is one they can reach.

02 / 07

Name the device by what it does

A standing bed is not a product most people have seen, and a bedside bathing system is not a product most people have a word for. So the products are named for their function, and the shop card carries a category label that says who it is for. A caregiver can tell what the bathing system does from its name alone, and the About page describes each device in plain language before the buyer meets any manufacturer’s vocabulary.

Define the words before selling into them

Adaptive, assistive, mobility aid: the vocabulary is contested and the buyer may not share it. Six blog articles do the sorting, defining adaptive devices as tools that modify how a person interacts with their environment and assistive devices as support without altering the task. A reader who did not know the difference leaves knowing, with the category pages linked from the sentence that explains them, and every article ends by inviting a consultation.

Attribute every standard with care

In this category the difference between a product claim and a company certification is the one a compliance officer reads. The FAQ names international standards, a safety test and documentation, all as statements about the product and its manufacturer, with the documents linked. This page repeats them in that relationship and no stronger. It does not say FairPath is certified, registered or approved under anything, because the site does not say so.

Hold the site to its own standard

A supplier of assistive devices is judged on its own site’s accessibility before anything else, and fairly. The site carries a skip link and describes its product image by name and function. This page claims no more than that: no accessibility audit was run, no conformance level is asserted, and the observations are of the pages as published rather than a test with a screen reader or a keyboard.

What's next

Three ways to finish the job

The structure answers the institutional buyer’s questions more completely than most assistive-device sites. The next layer makes its most valuable facts readable by everyone.

  • Give each product its own listing and termsMost published terms name the standing bed. List both products with their own pages and put the warranty, delivery and return terms on each as well as in the FAQ.
  • Attribute each standard in one lineAdd “as documented by the manufacturer” beside each reference, so a hurried visitor does not read a product claim as the company’s own.
  • Publish the capability statement as text and PDFThe document an institutional buyer would forward should be readable by a screen reader, a search engine and a buyer at once, alongside an accessibility statement that says what has been checked.

Questions

About this assistive devices case study

What does FairPath Consultants offer?

Adaptive and assistive devices, as the site states: a standing bed and a portable bedside shower system, with consultation, white-glove delivery, a stated warranty, maintenance and a buy-back membership. It serves individuals, caregivers and buyers in the government, healthcare and education sectors, and is based in Gardena, California.

What is the difference between adaptive and assistive devices?

The site’s blog defines adaptive devices as tools that modify the way an individual interacts with their environment or performs a specific task, and assistive devices as support without altering the task itself. The two are the site’s product categories.

Does the site claim the devices are medically approved or the company certified?

No. Standards and documentation appear as statements about the product and its manufacturer, with documents linked, and this case study repeats them in that relationship only. No certification of FairPath, medical benefit, insurance or Medicare coverage, or accessibility conformance level is claimed.
