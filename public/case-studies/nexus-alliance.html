The business

The offer is nuanced. The site is where it becomes a position a prime can repeat

Nexus Alliance Inc. supports prime contractors that are executing federal contracts, in operational areas where the prime’s own experience is limited. Its website is one page and a terms page, and it opens with a question rather than a claim. The page description names the field as specialised waste management, and the footer lists seven NAICS codes.

Reduce that to a subcontractor and the position disappears. A subcontractor bids for a slice of the work. The published offer is different in kind: the prime keeps the contract, the relationship and the lead, and Nexus brings the operational system the requirement needs. The site says so in one sentence: “Instead of competing for the same work, we become an extension of your operational team.”

Two readers, one scroll

  • →The prime’s programme lead reads to solve a problem: an awarded contract with a requirement the team has not run before.
  • →The contracts or procurement officer reads to check the company is a legitimate partner: its codes, its name, how to reach it.
  • →A pitch serves only the first, a capability statement only the second. This page is built for both.

The challenge

A supporting role that must not sound secondary

A company that defines itself by what it will not do is at constant risk of sounding like less than a company. The site has to make the supporting role read as strength, say what the support is for, and give a procurement officer the identifiers they look for. Five pressures shaped the build.

The role is defined by what it is not

Not a competitor, not a replacement, not a dependency. Three negatives in a row read as a disclaimer unless each is paired with what the company is.

The buyer has already won

A prime reading this has the award in hand. It is not looking for help bidding; it is looking for help performing. The page has to meet the reader after the win.

The prime is protective of its position

A partner who looks like a rival is a risk to the prime’s relationship with the agency. The first screen has to remove that fear.

Federal readers look for identifiers

NAICS codes, an entity name, a way to reach the company. A page without them is not a federal-market page, however good the prose.

Claims in this market must be exact

Agencies, awards, clearances and set-asides are checkable, and the company cannot name its clients. Credibility has to come from precision, not a logo wall.

The organising principle: open with the prime’s own question, answer it with one sentence that defines the role as an extension rather than a competitor, pair every “we don’t” with a “we do,” prove the role with a scenario a prime will recognise, put the NAICS codes where a procurement reader looks, and make the only ask a conversation about fit.

What we built

01 / 07

One question, one role, one conversation

A single linear page: the prime’s question, a one-sentence vision, four paired statements, an anonymised scenario, seven NAICS codes and a short form with its compliance published behind it. There is no navigation to compete with the scroll, which on a one-page site is correct. By the time a prime sends the form, it has an award with a requirement it has not run before, has read a page that names that situation, has checked that the company will not threaten its position, and has decided a conversation costs it nothing.

02 / 07

Open with the prime’s own question

Most B2B sites open with a claim. This one opens with the doubt the reader walked in with, set in large type, and spends the page answering it. The line beneath it, “You won the contract, now let’s make it work,” names the reader’s situation before it names the company. A prime that arrives sceptical is met by its own scepticism, which is disarming in a way a claim cannot be.

03 / 07

Define the role by contrast

The role is defined by what it is not, which is a risk: three negatives in a row read as a disclaimer. The page pairs every “we don’t” with a “we do” in the same breath, so the reader never sits with a negative for longer than a line. The contrast is with the prime’s fear, not with a competitor, which is why it reads as strength.

Prove it with a scenario a prime recognises

The company cannot or chooses not to name clients, so proof is one anonymised scenario: a prime that entered a service area where it lacked specialised operational experience, and a partner that helped establish an operational system. 05 / 07

Put the market’s identifiers where a buyer looks

A procurement reader checks the company before the programme lead does. The footer lists seven NAICS codes where that reader expects to find them, and the page uses the market’s own vocabulary correctly: prime contractor, subcontractor, requirement, execution. Publishing codes states the industries a company works in. It is not a qualification, and nothing on this page says it is.

06 / 07

End on a conversation, not a quote

There is no price list, so there is no quote request. The contact block opens with a qualifying question, “Have you been awarded a federal contract where additional operational expertise could strengthen execution?”, which tells the wrong reader to leave and the right one to write. Behind the short form sits a published SMS policy, a privacy policy and terms, which is the compliance a federal reader notices.

07 / 07

Let restraint do the work

In a market where awards, agencies and registrations can be checked, a site that implies more than it holds is worse than one that says less. This one names no agency, award, customer, set-aside status or certification, and the page does not either. The restraint is the credential: a prime that is putting its own standing behind a partner can see that nothing here will be contradicted by a search.

What's next

Three ways to make the position concrete

The positioning is the clearest statement of a supporting role in this market. The next layer says what the support consists of and makes the proof readable.

  • Say what the support consists ofThe page defines the role and never lists the work. A sentence naming the operational areas, and three or four examples of what an operational system looks like, would let a prime say “under this code, doing this.”
  • Give the identifiers a procurement reader needsAdd the official title beside each NAICS code, the entity’s details and, where the company holds them, its registrations exactly as registered, plus a one-page capability statement a prime can forward.
  • Make the proof readableName partners in text where permission exists, add descriptions to every image, and give the experience claim its noun: years of what. A second anonymised scenario, with a sector and a scale, would stop one story carrying the whole case.

About this federal contract support case study

What does Nexus Alliance do?

It supports prime contractors that are executing federal contracts, in operational areas where the prime’s own experience is limited. The site defines the role as an extension of the prime’s operational team. It does not list specific services or tasks, and the one operational field it names, specialised waste management, appears in the page description.

Is Nexus Alliance a subcontractor?

The site does not present it as one. It says the company does not compete for the prime’s role and that the prime leads while Nexus strengthens. It mentions working with prime contractors and subcontractors, but describes no subcontract or teaming arrangement.

Does Nexus Alliance hold federal registrations or certifications?

None is published on the site, and this case study claims none. The only federal identifiers on the page are seven NAICS codes, which state the industries the company works in. They are not a qualification.
