The business

Breadth is the asset. The site decides whether it reads as depth

Amins Consult is a software engineering consultancy based in Scranton, Pennsylvania. The home page states the scope in one sub-headline, “Engineering the Future of Regulated Industries”, and names the industries on the same screen: Federal, Healthcare and Finance.

Reduce that to “we build software” and the firm disappears into a category with thousands of entrants. The published capability runs far wider than a development shop: six service pillars, three industry sections, and a seven-category technology stack naming tools from LangChain and Palantir Foundry to Kafka and Power BI. The site cannot win by asserting range. It has to organise it so a buyer can check it.

Who reads the page

  • →A federal program director reading for compliance vocabulary.
  • →A healthcare technology leader reading for interoperability and PHI handling.
  • →A fintech engineering lead reading for payment rails and fraud.
  • →A procurement lead comparing three firms in the same afternoon.
  • →A technical evaluator forwarded the link for a second opinion.

The challenge

Six disciplines, three industries, one page to read them

Enterprise buyers screen for security posture before they read about capability, and the person who finds the site is rarely the person who signs. Five pressures shaped the build.

The range is wide and hard to name

AI agents, DevSecOps, data mesh, healthcare interoperability, federal mission systems, fintech platforms. Listed flat, it reads unfocused; left unexplained, it looks like a body shop with a long menu.

Three industries, three vocabularies

A federal reader wants IL5. A healthcare reader wants FHIR. A fintech reader wants ISO 20022. One page cannot sound fluent in all three unless it is structured to.

Buying is slow and multi-handed

The page is forwarded, read in procurement and compared against two others. It has to survive being read by someone who was not looking for it.

Security is screened first

A site that mentions compliance once, on a dedicated page, has already lost the reader who needed it on the first screen.

Credibility must be shown

“Experienced, secure, trusted” survive no comparison. Only named standards, named tools and stated engagement models do.

The organising principle: let the reader self-identify by industry first, put the capability under that industry in the reader's own regulatory vocabulary, repeat the security message everywhere a buyer would look, and make first contact feel like opening a partnership rather than submitting a lead.

What we built

01 / 07

Industry, then discipline, then proof

Each layer of the site answers the question the previous one raises: for whom, doing what, how deep, can I check it, how do I buy. One sub-headline, six cards, three industry sections, a seven-block stack and a single contact route, arranged so a reader can stop at any depth and still have the answer to what they came for.

02 / 07

Let the reader self-identify by industry

Service cards are ordered by sector first, and each pairs a regulated context with the standard or platform a buyer would already be searching for. Three industry sections on the services page then speak each sector's own language: HEDIS and HCC for healthcare, Basel III and SAR for finance, DRRS and JADC2 for federal. A site written for one reader reads as a stranger to the other two. This one carries all three without any of them noticing the others being served.

04 / 07

Security on the first screen

In these sectors a buyer's first filter is posture, not skill. The site repeats the security message everywhere a buyer would look instead of parking it on one compliance page. It is in the hero, in the regulated-industry service cards, in the first of three trust pillars, “Security First, Always”, and in the FAQ answer that calls security the “Day 0” priority. The buyer who filters on compliance first never has to go looking.

Where the security message appears

  • →The hero and its regulated-industries sub-headline.
  • →Every regulated-industry card on the home and services pages.
  • →The first trust pillar, Zero Trust and automated compliance from Day One.
  • →The services FAQ, where security is the Day 0 priority.
05 / 07

AI as an engineering discipline

Every consultancy can put AI in a headline. Here it is one pillar of six, not the hero, which is the editorial decision that matches the firm's stated principle, “Engineering Logic, Not Hype”. The card names four disciplines, the About page lists the tools behind them, and inside the industry sections AI is tied to workflows a buyer can price: chart abstraction via LLMs and prior auth automation in healthcare, fraud detection in finance, computer vision for GEOINT in federal. The tool list is published as a statement of technologies the firm works with, not a record of any specific engagement.

07 / 07

One door, labelled for the buyer

Every call to action on the site leads to one contact page headed “Partner With Us”. The heading frames the first message as the opening of a relationship rather than a lead submission, and it closes the loop opened by the third trust pillar, “Partner, Not Just Vendor”. A four-field form keeps friction low, the email line is labelled for the inquiry type, and phone hours are stated, because an enterprise buyer wants to know a person answers before they write.

What's next

Three ways to route the right buyer

The positioning is sound. The next layer gives each inquiry more to route on and gives each sector more to read.

  • Give the inquiry form something to route onTwo optional fields, industry and engagement type, would let every inquiry arrive ready for the right person and match the three engagement models already published.
  • Publish for the four sectorsThe blog covers cloud, integration, platform engineering and PySpark. Articles on healthcare, federal, fintech and AI would meet buyers where the thesis says they search first.
  • Explain the federal-specific product namesA line of context on names such as Palantir Foundry would let that credential do its full work for healthcare and finance readers too.
