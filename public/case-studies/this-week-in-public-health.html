The product

Research is published faster than it can be read. The product is where volume becomes understanding, not a backlog

This Week in Public Health is a research intelligence product made by PubTrawlr, a Lancaster, Pennsylvania company. The home page title states the promise in five words: “Connecting you to public health research.” Behind it sit a library of research summaries, a blog of long-form analysis, a Thursday newsletter, an AI research assistant, a personalised issue builder and a two-tier subscription.

Reduce that to a newsletter and most of the product disappears. The published experience is a pipeline: journals are monitored, studies are filtered, findings are translated into plain language, and the reader is then offered three ways to go deeper: a full-text link, a cited AI answer, or a personalised issue built from their role and interests.

Two readers, one citation

  • →The practitioner reads for the takeaway: what did this study find, does it apply to my population.
  • →The researcher or educator reads for the evidence: which journal, which authors, can I cite it.
  • →The citation is the hinge between them, so the product is neither a news feed nor a database.

The challenge

Volume, jargon and credibility, all at once

Thousands of studies a week, written for peers, read by people with twenty minutes and a reputation to protect. Five pressures shaped the build.

Volume outruns attention

A reader cannot scan a library that size. Something has to choose, and the reader has to trust the choosing.

Academic writing is not written for the reader

A commissioner needs the finding, the population and the implication in three sentences, with the acronyms removed.

A summary without a citation is a rumour

In public health the reader will be asked “where did you read that.” Every plain sentence has to trace to a journal, an author and a year.

AI raises the credibility bar

Public-health readers know what hallucination means. An assistant that cannot show its sources is a liability on a site whose proposition is evidence.

Habit is the product

A research tool used once is a bookmark. The value compounds only if the reader returns every week, so the product needs a cadence, not just a library.

The organising principle: curate before the reader arrives, translate every finding into plain language with its citation attached, put the full-text link and the assistant one step from every summary, let the reader narrow the feed to their role and topic, and deliver it on a fixed day.

The article entry is the unit

Every entry in the library carries the same fields: source, date, plain-language title, authors, a short summary and a link. A reader who has read one entry can read a hundred, and the uniformity is what makes a daily-updated library navigable without filters. The summaries state who was studied, what was measured and why it matters, which is the three-part structure a practitioner needs and an abstract rarely provides in that order.

What each entry carries

  • 01A source tag and a date.
  • 02A title rewritten in plain language.
  • 03Authors, and a summary of the finding and why it matters.
  • 04A link to the paper.
04 / 07

Long-form posts that show the standard

The blog is where the product shows its evidence standard in full. A post carries a named author, section headings, a Sources section of APA references with DOI links to journals such as The Lancet Public Health and JAMA Internal Medicine, related posts and a comments thread. It is the format a researcher would cite and a commissioner would forward, and it demonstrates on a free page the citation discipline the paid tier promises everywhere.

05 / 07

A feed that fits, in three dropdowns

Personalisation here is not a recommendation engine; it is three dropdowns and an email address. “Build My Issue” asks for a work setting, one of eight topics and a role, and promises five findings with what was found and why it matters. That is the right scale for a first contact, and it produces something concrete a reader can evaluate on receipt. A rural-health practitioner and a hospital executive get different five studies from the same week, and the form travels into the long posts where readers already are.

The second question, answered with a source

The difference between a news feed and a research tool is what happens after the summary. The site positions the AI assistant as the second half of reading: follow-up questions on any paper with “instant, cited answers.” The FAQ states three commitments, stated before the reader has typed anything: every answer is cited, private queries are not used for training, and the assistant is evaluated for bias and hallucinations. This page describes the assistant only as the site does, and says nothing about how it is built.

About this public health news case study

What is This Week in Public Health?

A research intelligence product by PubTrawlr: plain-language summaries of peer-reviewed public-health studies, long-form analysis with cited sources, a Thursday newsletter, an AI research assistant and a personalised issue builder, with a free preview and a paid digest.

Is it a news service?

Not in the breaking-news sense. The site publishes dated summaries of peer-reviewed studies, “updated daily,” and promises “no hype.” The weekly digest arrives on Thursday mornings. Searches such as public health news describe how people look, and the page says what the product actually publishes.

Who is it written for?

The About page names researchers, government leaders and non-profits. In practice that is health department leads, programme directors and commissioners who read for the takeaway, and researchers and educators who read for the evidence and need a citation.
