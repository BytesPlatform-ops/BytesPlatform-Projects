The business

The buyer knows the requirement. The site decides whether it becomes a part number

Jobco Supply is an industrial heater and sensor supplier in Fort Smith, Arkansas. The home page states the business in its headline: “Electric Heaters, Temperature Sensors & Accessories for Today's Manufacturers.” Beneath it sit sixteen category tiles, a search box, a storefront with a cart, six manufacturers named, custom quote forms and a library of eighty-four technical documents.

Reduce that to a parts store and the business disappears into a category where every competitor lists the same heaters from the same factories. The published offer is wider than a catalog: stock cartridge heaters organised by diameter and voltage, band heaters purchasable by inside diameter, custom configurators for everything the tables do not cover, and a help page that answers procurement questions before anyone asks.

Two readers, one engineer

  • →The maintenance buyer with a machine down, reading for the exact part: diameter, insertion, voltage, watts, in stock, ships today.
  • →The design engineer speccing a new line, reading for options: which sheath survives the temperature, which lead fits the conduit.
  • →One architecture that serves a price list reader and a catalog reader without either noticing.

The challenge

Requirements, not part numbers

Buyers arrive with a diameter, a wattage, a voltage and an application. Sixteen heater families, six manufacturers and many variants have to resolve to one row or one form. Five pressures shaped the build.

The buyer has a spec, not a SKU

A half-inch bore, three inches deep, 240 volts. No search box answers that sentence, so the taxonomy has to be built from the attributes a buyer already has.

Each family has its own grammar

Cartridge heaters are chosen by diameter. Band heaters by inside diameter and width. Hot air heaters by brand line and kilowatts. One navigation pattern cannot serve all of them.

Stock and custom are different purchases

A stock part is bought in a minute from a table. A custom part is a conversation about sheath, lead, seal and fitting. Put them on one page and both buyers are slowed down.

Validation prevents the return

A heater a sixteenth of an inch oversize goes back with a restocking fee. Tolerances and sizing guidance have to be one click from the row.

The phone is a feature

In this category assisted buying is normal. Calling has to feel like the next step, not the fallback when the website failed.

The organising principle: split every family by the attribute its buyer knows first, put the specification on the row and the tolerances one click away, separate the stock table from the custom form, and put the phone number beside every decision as the honest third door.

02 / 07

Sixteen families, each with its own grammar

Cartridge heaters split seven ways by diameter because diameter is the thing a bore dictates, then by voltage because that is what a panel dictates. Band heaters split by voltage first, since a 480 V band on a 240 V panel is the expensive mistake, then by inside diameter from one inch to twelve. A buyer with a half-inch bore never sees a three-eighths heater. That is what taxonomy does for discoverability: it removes what cannot fit before the buyer has to read it.

The row does the validating

The half-inch cartridge page is a part-row table of insertion length, overall length, volts, watts, lead length, feature and part ID, grouped by lead length and running to roughly a hundred and fifty rows in its first group. A buyer reads it the way they read a catalog page, which is the point. A supplier that publishes overall length to the hundredth of an inch is a supplier that expects to be measured against, and a tolerances document is linked from the category above it.

04 / 07

Stock first, custom second

The category page asks the question in its own heading, “Do you need a custom cartridge heater?”, after it has shown the stock. The buyer is never sent to a form for a part that was on the shelf. When a heater does need to be made, six forms, one per heater type, walk the buyer through the options an applications engineer would ask about, in that order: sheath, lead wire, termination, seals, fittings. Engineering review is named as the next step, and a human call as the outcome.

A library that prevents the return

Every document is a reason the wrong part does not get ordered, and a landing page for a search no product listing would catch. Eighty-four guides, data sheets, manuals and drawings are grouped by family, with seals, leads, sheaths and tolerances for cartridge heaters, lead, conduit and clamp options for bands, and a Heater Sizing and Watt Density Calculator. A distributor that hosts the manufacturer's manual and a calculator is one that expects to be asked an engineering question.

07 / 07

Three doors, none disguised as another

Industrial commerce is assisted buying with a cart attached. A connector or a stock cartridge heater goes in the cart. A heater with a special sheath and a right-angle conduit goes on a form that engineering reviews. Everything else goes to a phone number that sits in the top bar of every page and in a home page block headed “Something Else?” that offers quotes on products not shown on the site. For a part that is not on the site at all, calling is the designed route, not the failure case.

What's next

Three ways to shorten the path to a part

The taxonomy understands half inch, 240 volt, cartridge. The next layer carries that same understanding to the other ways a buyer reaches the catalog.

  • Teach site search to read a specA query such as cartridge heater 240v should resolve to the matching diameter and voltage pages, the way the menus already do.
  • Put price and stock on the rowA price or an availability flag beside each part would close the gap between validating a part and learning what it costs.
  • Deepen the thinner familiesTemperature controllers have a category page and two documents, the thinnest coverage relative to how often buyers search for them.
