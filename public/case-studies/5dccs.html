The business

The service is specialised. The site is where it becomes a decision a buyer can make

Construction Containment Services, trading as 5DCCS, is a San Jose company that rents, installs, sells and removes modular temporary wall systems for construction inside occupied buildings. The home page title states the offer: “Modular Temporary Walls | San Jose.” It is sold into ten sectors with a page each, from healthcare and airports to data centres and federal facilities.

Reduce that to a wall rental and most of the site disappears. The published offer runs from full-service rental, where the company assesses, plans, installs, adjusts and removes, through self-service rental and outright sale, to containment consulting, framed by a device the company has made its name from: the 5 Ds.

Two readers, one quote

  • →The facility or project manager reads to keep a building open: can patients stay on the ward, what will the inspector want to see.
  • →The estimator or general contractor reads for the number: how fast, what labour, what terms.
  • →One architecture carries both to the same quote request.

The challenge

A category the buyer has to learn before they can buy

Modular containment is unfamiliar, compliance-sensitive and bought under deadline. The site has to educate without lecturing and claim without overclaiming. Five pressures shaped the build.

Most buyers do not know the category exists

They know the problem: renovate the third floor while the second stays open. The site has to be found by the problem and name the category on the first screen.

The alternative is what everyone already does

Plastic sheeting and temporary drywall are familiar, cheap and disposable. The site has to argue against a habit, not a competitor.

Compliance is the real purchase

In a hospital the containment is an infection-control permit condition. The site has to say what the system supports without saying more than it can prove.

Each sector has its own vocabulary

ICRA in healthcare, downtime in data centres, passenger flow in airports. A single services page speaks to none of them.

The job is local and the timeline is short

A San Jose tenant improvement needs a crew this week and someone who knows the city’s permit desk. The site has to prove both.

The organising principle: name the category on the first screen and give the buyer five words for the problem, sell four ways of working rather than one product, write a page for each sector in its own vocabulary and a page for each city with its permit desk named, state every standard in the exact relationship the company has to it, and put a quote request within one click of wherever the buyer is.

The 5 Ds, then four ways, then the sector, then the city

A memorable frame for the problem, four service models, ten sector pages, city pages with local permitting content, and a five-step process repeated on every page. By the time a project manager requests a quote, they have searched the problem, learned what the category is called, recognised their own fears in five words, read the sector page in their own vocabulary and seen walls standing in a building like theirs.

02 / 07

Five words for the problem

The 5 Ds turn a product nobody asked for into five problems everybody has: Dust, Debris, Decibels, Demolition and Disruption. A facility manager cannot picture a gasketed modular panel, but they can picture dust in a ward, debris in a corridor, noise through a wall, a demolition they cannot schedule and a disruption they will be blamed for. Five nouns that alliterate, in the company’s own name, let the buyer say what they are afraid of and make the company the answer to all five at once.

The argument against a habit

The competitor is not another vendor; it is plastic sheeting and temporary drywall, which are familiar, cheap to specify and disposable. The services page answers it with “Why Modular Walls Beat Plastic Sheeting and Drywall” and a comparison table, and the vision statement frames the walls as reusable alternatives that keep debris out of landfills. 06 / 07

The standard stated carefully

In this category the standard is the purchase. A hospital buys a barrier its infection-control officer can put in an ICRA permit, so the words matter. The healthcare page says systems are “specified to meet” ICRA Class IV and Class V requirements, and the About page says compliance documentation is available on request. This page uses that wording and nothing stronger: it does not say the company or its systems are certified, because ICRA is a process a facility runs, not a certificate a vendor holds.

What the page does and does not say

07 / 07

The permit desk, named

The San Jose page is what makes the company local rather than regional. It names the neighbourhoods, nearby hospitals and the airport, and then does what no template does: it names the permit office, the fire department’s prevention bureau, the city’s expedited-review programme and a Cal/OSHA section, and answers “Do temporary walls require a permit in San Jose?” A contractor who has stood at that counter reads a company that has stood there too. What's next

Three ways to sharpen the frame

The structure is sound. The next layer makes the best idea in the site do more of the work.

  • Define each DThe five words appear as a list and a mission line. A sentence on what the company does about dust that it does not do about decibels would turn the frame into a specification.
  • Build the other city pages from the San Jose modelThe permit-desk pattern is the strongest proof of locality on the site. The remaining Northern California cities are the largest piece of unbuilt structure.
  • Carry the healthcare wording everywhere a standard is named“Specified to meet,” with documentation on request, is the precise form. Every page an inspector might read should use it.
